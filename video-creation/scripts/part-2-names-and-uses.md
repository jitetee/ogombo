## S1 HOOK [G: mystery + stakes]
"The surgeon says, give me a Kocher, and you hand over the wrong clamp. Nothing dramatic happens. No alarm, no shouting. But a clamp with teeth is now biting tissue that was supposed to stay, and the only thing that could have told you was something you can see in half a second. So here is the question. Two clamps look almost the same on the tray, one is gentle and one bites, so how do you tell which is which when your hands are already moving?"

## S3 CLAMPS BY NAME [P]
"Start with the clamps, because this is where the half second lives. The Crile hemostat is atraumatic, which means no teeth, and it grasps vessels that will be tied off. The Kelly clamp is the same idea but larger, for larger tissue or vessels. Now the Kocher. The Kocher is traumatic, it has teeth, and it holds tissue that is going to be removed. So the answer is in the jaws. Teeth means it is allowed to hurt tissue, no teeth means it is not. And the Allis and the Babcock have slightly rounded jaws, and both are used to grasp intestine. So remember this: locking clamps can be traumatic or atraumatic, check the jaws."
Card: crile-kelly-kocher-and-allis-clamps (ring it, write CRILE / KELLY / KOCHER / ALLIS next to each, underline NO TEETH vs TEETH).
D1 card image revealed. D2 red ring on the card. D3 handwritten labels on the card. D4 green tick icon next to Crile (smooth jaw). D5 red cross/tooth icon next to Kocher. D6 yellow highlight on "check the jaws". D7 narrator tapping the card.

## S4 THE JAW HABIT [G -> P]
"But, here is the catch, because I told you the answer was a habit and so far I have only given you three names. So the habit is this. Every time you pick up a clamp, look at the jaws before the name. Teeth or no teeth, smooth or serrated, rounded or sharp. The name tells you what someone called it a hundred years ago. The jaws tell you what it will do to the tissue tonight. Think of it like biting an apple with your teeth, and then holding a baby's hand with your gums. Both are grips. One is allowed to hurt, and the other must never. Now picture the surgeon holding out a hand without looking, because their eyes are on the wound. They trust you to read the jaws for them. That is the real job of the scrub."
D1 narrator with a magnifier. D2 giant eye icon. D3 three jaw close-ups drawn: smooth, serrated, toothed. D4 arrow from each jaw to a word: gentle, grips, bites. D5 big underline under JAWS FIRST. D6 clock stopped at half a second.

## S5 ARTERY FORCEPS [P, then G for the next]
"Now the artery forceps, and there are a lot of names here, so let's sort them. Halstead Mosquito is the standard pattern for stopping bleeding, with a box joint. Cushing is fine with half-serrated jaws, one hundred and forty millimetres long. Adson is one hundred and eighty, and Cairns is one hundred and forty-five with half-serrated jaws. Then Crile has serrated jaws, Dunhill has fine serrations, Lawson Tait has a straight jaw, and Kocher has one or two teeth. Spencer Wells and Rochester-Pean both have a box joint. What do you take from all that? Artery forceps control bleeding from vessels, they are not needle holders, and because they ratchet closed, open the ratchet before you clean them. Now, teeth hold firmly, but teeth can damage tissue, so which one would you reach for on a delicate vessel? Hold that thought."

D1-D3 the three cards. D4 ratchet drawn open with a hand opening it. D5 red banner "NOT NEEDLE HOLDERS". D6 length ruler marks 140 / 145 / 180. D7 question mark over the toothed Kocher.

## S7 TISSUE FORCEPS [G: problem]
"So you have clamped and tied. But, now the surgeon needs to hold tissue, and here is the problem: tissue ranges from skin to fascia to bowel, and one forceps cannot be right for all three. So the rule is to match the forceps to the tissue. Fine for delicate, heavy for fascia."
D1 narrator holding two forceps. D2 three tissue sketches: skin, fascia, bowel. D3 arrows from forceps to each. D4 scale from FINE to HEAVY. D5 big handwritten "MATCH THE TISSUE". D6 scalpel mark on skin.

## S8 NAMED TISSUE FORCEPS [P]
"Adson is toothed at the tip and handles dense tissue like skin closures. Bonney is the heavy one, for thick tissue like a fascial closure. DeBakey and Russian are atraumatic, which means they grasp tissue during dissection without biting it. And in the intestine group, Allis, Duval, Babcock and Shaw all have a box joint, Babcock has serrated jaws, and the Allis and Babcock have rounded jaws for grasping intestine, so, be gentle."
Cards: adson-bonney-debakey-and-russian-forceps, allis-duval-babcock-and-shaw-tissue-forceps.
D1-D2 two cards. D3 skin sketch + Adson tip. D4 fascia sketch + Bonney. D5 bowel sketch + Babcock rounded jaws. D6 "BE GENTLE" speech bubble.

## S9 DISSECTING FORCEPS [P]
"For fine dissecting, Gillies has one or two teeth. Lane's has one or two teeth on serrated jaws and is one hundred and forty millimetres. Dunhill is light with fine serrated jaws, and Fredrick Coole has extra fine serrated jaws. McIndoe is one hundred and fifty-two millimetres, and a modified one has a one millimetre tip for fine, powerful dissection. Jean has serrated jaws, and Charnley-McIndoe is one hundred and fifty. Two rules. Fine tips for fine tissue, because too much regripping can damage tissue, and hold them in a pen grip for the best control."
Cards: gillies-lanes-dunhill-and-coole-dissecting-forceps, mcindoe-jean-and-charnley-dissecting-forceps.
D1-D2 cards. D3 hand in pen grip (drawn). D4 tip zoom 1 mm circle. D5 red cross "NO REGRIPPING". D6 ruler 140 / 150 / 152.

## S10 PAUSE QUIZ 1 [G]
"Pause and answer. The surgeon is closing fascia, a thick strong layer. Which forceps do you hand over? A, Dunhill, the light fine one. B, Bonney, the heavy one. Or C, a Babcock. Pause now. ... The answer is B. Heavy for fascia. If you said that, you are already matching the tool to the tissue."
D1 narrator with a quiz board. D2 three option cards A B C. D3 big "PAUSE" stamp. D4 timer ring. D5 green tick on B. D6 fascia layer sketch.

## S11 SCISSORS [G: counterintuitive]
"Now scissors, and here is something that surprises most students: the same name does not always mean the same job. Mayo is heavy. Straight Mayo cuts suture, curved Mayo cuts heavy tissue such as fascia. Metzenbaum, we call it Metz, is lighter. It cuts delicate tissue and does blunt dissection. Pott's is a fine scissor for making incisions in blood vessels, and Iris is fine for dissection and fine suture, made for eye surgery and now multipurpose. So the rule is easy, heavy scissors for heavy tissue, light scissors for delicate tissue."
Card: mayo-metzenbaum-potts-and-iris-scissors.
D1 card. D2 straight Mayo with suture. D3 curved Mayo on fascia. D4 Metz on delicate tissue. D5 balance scale HEAVY / LIGHT. D6 eye drawn near Iris.

## S12 FINE AND UTILITY [P]
"The fine pattern scissors are Littler, with an eye for suture, Kilner, the ligature stitch scissors and McIndoe at one hundred and seventy-eight millimetres, and the rule is keep them off heavy materials. Then Fergusson is angled on the flat, Paynes cuts sutures, and the utility scissors have a black handle. And the dressing scissors, Lister bandage scissors with angled blades, Mayo-Harrington and the plain dressing scissors, all have a tungsten carbide hard insert. They cut dressings. Do not use them in surgery, because the edge that is perfect for a bandage is wrong for tissue, and once a scissor has been used on the wrong material, nobody on the table can tell by looking."
Cards: fine-pattern-dissecting-scissors, fergusson-paynes-and-utility-scissors, dressing-and-bandage-scissors.
D1-D3 cards. D4 bandage with angled scissors. D5 big red "NOT IN SURGERY". D6 tungsten insert zoom. D7 suture thread cut.

## S13 BLACK BOWS [G -> P]
"Here's a trick for the rest. Look at the handles. Black bows mean supercut scissors: Boyd, blunt blunt, Iris, and supercut dressing and Mayo with tungsten carbide. So the colour tells you what is in your hand before you read a word."
Card: supercut-scissors-black-handles.
D1 card. D2 black bows circled. D3 colour swatch black = supercut. D4 gold swatch (previews S15). D5 narrator pointing at handles. D6 "COLOUR = CLUE" banner.

## S14 NEEDLE HOLDERS [P]
"Needle holders next. Mayo is box joint and straight and has a ratchet to stop the needle slipping, and it works for right and left handers. Crile-Wood is straight, baby, fine. Gillies is the heavy pattern at one hundred and fifty-eight millimetres, with no ratchet, so holding the needle takes practice. And Olsen-Hegar has a screw joint and is a combined scissor and needle holder. Then Kilner has cross-serrated jaws, MacPhail has one copper jaw, Mathieu has a screw joint, and Naughton Morgan has box joints with cross-serrated jaws. But, a worn needle holder lets the needle twist, so check the jaws. Always. And that matters, because a needle that twists in the jaws puts the stitch in the wrong place, and the surgeon has to stop, take it out and start again."
Cards: mayo-crile-wood-gillies-and-olsen-hegar-needle-holders, kilner-macphail-mathieu-and-naughton-morgan-needle-holders.
D1-D2 cards. D3 needle in jaws (drawn). D4 ratchet close-up. D5 scissor+holder combo for Olsen-Hegar. D6 twisted needle red cross. D7 magnifier on jaws.

## S15 GOLD HANDLES [P]
"And remember the colour clue. Gold bows identify tungsten carbide needle holders. Halsey, Derf and Ryder all have a tungsten carbide insert, and DeBakey is two hundred and fifty millimetres long. So gold in your hand means a hard insert, which is why these grip the needle so well."
Card: tungsten-carbide-needle-holders-gold-handles.
D1 card. D2 gold bows circled. D3 tungsten insert zoom. D4 colour chart: black = supercut, gold = tungsten. D5 ruler 250 mm. D6 narrator with a thumbs up.

## S16 THE SCALPEL [G: safety stakes]
"Now the one that can hurt you. Scalpel handles. The standard handle is one hundred and twenty-seven millimetres. The Baron handle is one hundred and thirty. And blades fit only certain handles, so if the blade will not click, stop. And the rule is, load and remove blades with forceps, never with your fingers."
Card: scalpel-handles-baron-and-standard.
D1 card. D2 blade on handle, forceps loading it. D3 big red cross on a bare finger. D4 forceps tick. D5 ruler 127 / 130. D6 warning triangle.

## S17 TOWEL CLIPS [G: secret]
"There is one clip that carries a secret, so tell the anaesthetist before you place it. Backhaus has a box joint and holds drapes in place on the patient. Moynihan has a screw joint, two-two teeth, for the sides of an incision. Jones is spring forceps type, and Shardles is spring, curved, cross action. The reason you tell the anaesthetist is that it is the first painful stimulus."
Card: towel-clips-backhaus-moynihan-jones-shardles.

## S19 PAUSE QUIZ 2 [G]
"One more pause. You pick up an unknown clamp and see teeth on the jaws. What do you assume? A, it is atraumatic. B, it holds tissue that will be removed. Or C, it is for the intestine. Pause. ... B. Teeth means traumatic, like the Kocher, for tissue that will be removed. And now you know the habit."

## S20 RECAP + HABIT [P]
"So here is the whole thing on one line. Look at the jaws first, match the tool to the tissue, use scissors only for what they were made to cut, and let the colour tell you the rest. Black bows are supercut, gold bows are tungsten carbide."
